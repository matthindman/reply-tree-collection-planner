How to read this

  • A cascade is the posts sharing one conversation ID (a root and its replies at any depth) plus every retweet and quote of those posts. Its size leaves the root out. A quote that is not a reply counts once in the cascade it quotes and is the root of a cascade of its own.
  • A plan has three steps. Search returns every root whose own retweet count passes a floor, or whose reply count does when a floor on replies is set. For the roots that look large enough, the replies are read: each reply carries its own counts, and their sum is the cascade's size. Then cascades at or above the first size are collected in full, and cascades at or above the second get their replies and quotes.
  • Sizes, replies and quotes are counts from 30 daily files for September 2026, for every cascade of 100+ posts, at 24, 48, 72 and 168 hours. A file is dated by the day its roots' seven days end, so the roots were posted from 25 August to 23 September; the two days with every post on file are the files of 9 and 10 September, for roots posted on 2 and 3 September. The page does not hold those cascades. It works on 10,000 simulated cascades, drawn from summary statistics of groups of at least 100 real ones (counts, totals and histograms by size, kind and class), so no row is a real cascade. They give the measured counts and post totals to within about 1%, and to within a few percent above 20,000 posts or for cascades rooted at a quote; the plans they give are within about 1% of plans worked on the real cascades.
  • Cascades rooted at a quote are counted from 100 posts up. A quote posted late in its parent's week was followed for less than seven days, so their sizes are lower bounds.
  • The daily files do not hold a root's own counts. They are drawn from a model fitted to the two days for which every post is on file (roots posted on 2 and 3 September). On those two days the model gives the share of cascades found by a pair of floors to within 3 points, and the number of roots returned to within 6%.
  • In a reply-led cascade at least half of the retweets are retweets of replies, so the root's own counts are a small part of its size (about a seventh, against 96% for other cascades). They are about a quarter of posts in cascades of 100+, most of them between 1,000 and 10,000 posts, and rare above 10,000. Their share ran from 23% to 35% of posts for roots posted up to 13 September and near 13% for the last roots in the files. Which cascades of the 30 days are reply-led is set by a classifier trained on the two full days; it is right for 97% of cascades on the day it did not see.
  • In reply-led cascades the replies arrive in bursts within the same second, and each is retweeted about 10 times and almost never more than 40. A sample was checked on X in November 2026. The replies are Chinese-language advertisements for sexual services, posted and retweeted by automated accounts under popular Chinese-language posts. None of 100 sampled replies was still visible, and all 25 whose text could be recovered were stock advertising lines. The post at the top is usually an ordinary post whose author has no part in it. One or two in a hundred of these replies are ordinary: threads and fan replies in Japanese. The plan shown first collects a random 2% of them, drawn by root ID before anything is read, so that the class can be weighted back to its full size; a floor of 100 replies on the root is what finds them. With the share set to none, that floor is not used: on the two full days a search by 500 retweets alone returned 98% of the other cascades of 1,000+ posts and 1% of the advertising ones. Reply-led cascades that are not advertising, about 1% of posts in cascades of 100+, are sampled or left out with them. The 1% stream carries their retweets either way.
  • A prize draw is a post that tells readers to follow, repost, reply or quote in order to enter a draw or receive a reward. In a topic study of 3,974 sampled roots for September 2026 they start 3% of the cascades that are not reply-led, 49% of those of 10,000 to 19,999 posts and 73% of those of 20,000+, and they hold 63% of the posts in cascades of 10,000+. Nearly all are run by companies (96% of those sampled). On the two full days their retweets are timed exactly as chance predicts for their rate and fall off at night, so the participants are people, drawn by the prize. The plan shown first collects a random 2% of them. A prize draw is recognised from the root’s text, which search returns with the root; one that is left out is not read or fetched. Which simulated cascades are prize draws is set by size alone, from the study’s shares, so the plans show what leaving them out frees, not which cascades they are.
  • Reading replies by counts: a root with more than five replies for each retweet is read as reply-led, with a size of its counts plus 4.25 times its replies; any other root is read as its counts alone. By stream: the size is 100 times the cascade's posts in the 1% sample, which needs each sampled post matched to its cascade; that has not been tried.
  • Finding by the stream is not offered as a plan. The 1% stream samples at least one post of nearly every cascade of 300+ posts, so it could find the cascades the floors miss, and it is the natural check on them. Its cost cannot be worked out here: every cascade under 100 posts with a post or two in the stream would also have to be looked up, and those cascades are not in the files.
  • Recognising a reply-led root: the same five-to-one rule, applied to the root's counts when search returns it. On the two days with every post on file it catches 80 to 83% of the reply-led roots that search returns and wrongly flags about 4% of the others (about 6% on this page's simulated rows). A reply-led root it misses is read and then dropped; a root it wrongly flags is set aside unless it is in the sample. The budget does not lean on the rule: if it caught no reply-led root at all, the opening plan would cost the same to within 0.1M units, because an unflagged reply-led root looks small from its counts and is not read. What would be lost is the reply-led sample.
  • What a sample can stand for: the reply-led sample is drawn from the roots search returns. With floors of 300 retweets or 100 replies those were a quarter of all reply-led cascades of 100+ posts on the two measured days: 26% of those from 300 to 999 posts at three days, 81 to 88% from 1,000 to 2,999, and all from 3,000 up. Over the whole month the 300 to 999 figure is nearer 40%, as this page shows: for roots posted from 14 September reply-led cascades carried about twice as many replies for their size, and 50 to 70% of those of 300 to 999 posts passed the floor of 100 replies. Weights from the sample apply to the returned roots only; an independent route, such as the 1% stream, is needed to measure the rest.
  • Costs are in units. One unit is the charge for one post returned. Each search pass is charged one unit for every root returned, taken as every root that passes a floor by the end of the window. Replies and other posts that pass the same floors, and lookups, are not costed. A shorter window is taken to scale a cascade's replies, quotes and root counts in step with its size.
  • Roots whose cascades never reached 100 posts are not in the files. A floor below 100 would return some of them; the floors offered here do not.
  • The stream-only design predicts at day 1 which cascades will pass 10,000 posts in seven days. Depending on the rule it finds most, not all, of the 3,206 and downloads 69M to 99M posts a month, wrong guesses included. Every post of all 3,206 is 60M. These figures are for seven days and original-post roots.
  • The first N retweets and a random sample of retweets are ways to buy some retweeters for the second tier. Neither has been tried, and search returns the newest posts first, so either may cost more than shown.
  • Nothing here has been tested on a paid account. That search filters on a post's counts at the time of the query, that a post's reply count is its direct replies, that the API's counts match the archive's, and what a repeated pass is charged, are assumed. A month with more volume than September, or 31 days, costs more: set the change in volume to see it. Other months scale September by their total.
  • How much the cost moves: worked on the real cascades day by day, the opening plan costs 2.1M units for an average day's roots, from 1.7M to 2.6M, and 63M for the 30 days. The dearest seven days in a row (roots of 8 to 14 September) ran at a rate of 68M a month. One month has been measured, so how much months differ is not known; the reserve is the allowance for that.

The model behind the conversations

The planner generates every conversation at random from one model with three parts. The charts below change as you move the model controls on the left.

An original post (root) draws its direct replies

Most roots that get any reply get exactly one. A small popular group gets tens, hundreds or thousands. Roots in the popular group sets how many roots are in that group; Reach of the most popular posts sets how large they get.

Each reply is answered, or not

A reply that gets an answer usually gets exactly one, and chains of single answers make a back-and-forth exchange. The chance is lower under a popular root and varies between conversations. The two get an answer controls and Difference between conversations set it.

Now and then an exchange splits

When an exchange stops, its last reply sometimes gets several replies at once. Each starts a new exchange. Side threads sets how often.

The equations

  1. Direct replies to the root, K. K = ⌊eZ⌋. With probability w the root is ordinary and Z is Normal with mean −a1/t12 and standard deviation 1/t1; otherwise it is popular and Z is Normal with mean −a2/t22 and standard deviation 1/t2. Only Z ≥ 0 is kept, so K ≥ 1.
  2. How persistent the conversation is, θ. θ is Normal(0, 1), drawn once per conversation. Write x = ln K.
  3. A reply to the root gets exactly one reply with probability q1 = logistic(c0 − c1x + s1θ).
  4. A later reply, in exchange j, gets exactly one reply with probability q2 = logistic(μ − γx + σθ + τεj), where εj is Normal(0, 1), drawn once per exchange.
  5. When an exchange ends, its last reply splits with probability φ = κ / (1 + ζ(α)) into D ≥ 2 replies, with P(D ≥ d) = (d − 1)−α. After a reply to the root, κ = logistic(g0 + f1x) and α = 1 + (α0 − 1)K−b1. After a later reply, κ = logistic(f0 + f1x) and α = 1 + (α0 − 1)K−b2.

logistic(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z). ζ is the Riemann zeta function, so κ is the average number of new exchanges started when one ends. It is always below 1, which keeps every conversation finite.

How to read this

  • Conversations are drawn from the reply-tree model described above. The page opens on a working guess for Japanese X today. It is not a measurement: no measurement of Japanese reply trees after 2011 was found.
  • Charges are in units. One unit is the charge for one post on X's public price list; counts and other charges are converted at the prices on the left. Whether they draw on your quota depends on the contract. Nothing here has been tested on a paid account.
  • Monthly volume and conversation shape rest on different counts. The default volume is an estimate of Japanese-written replies; the shapes describe conversations under Japanese roots.
  • The plan shown is the best of those tried: root search, a reply sample, or both; with or without a size check; keeping every flagged conversation or a random share. Every plan downloads what it keeps in full, or up to the read limit. Skipping the very largest conversations would collect more conversations and miss the largest ones; no plan here does that. Sizes and thresholds are tried on a grid, so “smallest size” means the smallest size tried.
  • The model is static. A conversation is judged on its final size; waiting times, deletions and replies not tagged as Japanese are not modelled.
  • The best guess and its variants come from two desk reviews (GPT Pro and Claude Opus). In the second round each was given the first round’s targets, so their agreement is partly built in. Scenarios are what-if cases, not probability bounds. Low end and High end change only the share of popular roots, which also changes how many conversations are quiet-root.
  • Moving a model control starts a fresh simulation, whose results carry a few percent of sampling noise. The scenario buttons load larger, steadier runs.
