    How to read this

    • A cascade is the posts sharing one conversation ID (a root and its replies at any depth) plus every retweet and quote of those posts. Its size leaves the root out. A quote that is not a reply counts once in the cascade it quotes and is the root of a cascade of its own.
    • A plan searches for roots whose own retweets or replies pass a floor, reads the replies of those that look large enough (each reply carries its own counts, and their sum is the cascade's size), then collects cascades at or above the first size in full and gives those at or above the second their replies and quotes.
    • The plan shown first takes every post of cascades of 10,000+ posts and the replies and quotes of cascades down to 100 posts, searching with floors of 100 retweets or 100 replies. It keeps a random 2% of reply-led roots and a random 20% of prize draws, the largest share offered that fits under the cap with the reserve kept back. Search finds 68% of the cascades of 100 to 299 posts, 97% of those of 300 to 999, and nearly all larger ones.
    • The data. Sizes, replies and quotes are counts from 30 daily files for September 2026, for every cascade of 100+ posts, at 24, 48, 72 and 168 hours. A file is dated by the day its roots' seven days end, so the roots were posted from 25 August to 23 September. Every post is on file for two days, called the two full days here: the files of 9 and 10 September, for roots of 2 and 3 September. Cascades rooted at a quote are counted from 100 posts up; a quote posted late in its parent's week was followed for less than seven days, so their sizes are lower bounds. Roots whose cascades never reached 100 posts are not in the files. A floor of 50 replies would return some of them, and they are not counted here.
    • The simulated cascades. The page does not hold the real cascades. It works on 10,000 simulated ones, drawn from summary statistics of groups of at least 100 real cascades (counts, totals and histograms by size, kind and class), so no row is a real cascade. They give the measured counts and post totals to within about 1% (a few percent above 20,000 posts or for cascades rooted at a quote), and plans within about 1% of plans worked on the real cascades.
    • A root's own counts are not in the daily files. For an original-post root, the shares of the retweets and of the replies that sit on the root are drawn together from the two full days' counts; for a quote root, from a fitted model. Fitted on one full day and tested on the other, the draw gives the share of cascades found by a pair of floors to within a point, and the number of roots returned to within 3%.
    • Reply-led cascades. At least half of the retweets are retweets of replies, so the root's own counts are a small part of the size (about a seventh, against 96% for other cascades). They are about a quarter of posts in cascades of 100+, most of them between 1,000 and 10,000 posts and rare above 10,000: 23% to 35% of posts for roots posted up to 13 September, and near 13% for the last roots in the files. Which cascades are reply-led is set by a classifier trained on the two full days; it is right for 97% of cascades on the day it did not see.
    • What they are. The replies arrive in bursts within the same second, and each is retweeted about 10 times and almost never more than 40. A sample checked on X in November 2026 shows Chinese-language advertisements for sexual services, posted and retweeted by automated accounts under popular Chinese-language posts whose authors usually have no part in it. None of 100 sampled replies was still visible, and all 25 whose text could be recovered were stock advertising lines. One or two replies in a hundred are ordinary threads and fan replies in Japanese. Reply-led cascades that are not advertising, about 1% of posts in cascades of 100+, are sampled or left out with the rest; the 1% stream carries their retweets either way.
    • The reply-led sample. The plan shown first keeps a random 2% of the reply-led roots search returns, drawn by root ID before anything is read; the floor of 100 replies is what finds them. With floors of 100 retweets or 100 replies, search returned a quarter of all reply-led cascades of 100+ posts on the two full days: 28% of those of 300 to 999 posts at three days, 82 to 89% of those of 1,000 to 2,999, and all from 3,000 up. Over the 30 files the 300 to 999 figure is nearer 40%: for roots posted from 14 September reply-led cascades carried about twice the replies for their size, and 50 to 70% of those of 300 to 999 posts passed the floor. So weights from the sample apply to returned roots only; measuring the rest needs an independent route, such as the 1% stream. With the share set to none, the floor on replies is not used: on the two full days a search by 500 retweets alone returned 98% of the other cascades of 1,000+ posts and 1% of the advertising ones.
    • Recognising a reply-led root. A root with more than five replies for each retweet is read as reply-led when search returns it. On the two full days, with floors of 100 retweets or 100 replies, the rule catches 78 to 81% of the reply-led roots search returns and wrongly flags about 2% of the others (84% and 2% on this page's simulated rows). A root it wrongly flags is set aside unless it is in the sample. A reply-led root it misses is read and then dropped: for the opening plan, about 5,100 roots and 1.6M units a month. The budget leans little on the rule: if it caught none, the opening plan would cost 0.5M units more, because an unflagged reply-led root mostly looks too small from its counts to be read. The sample is what would be lost.
    • Prize draws. A prize draw is a post that tells readers to follow, repost, reply or quote to enter a draw or receive a reward. In a topic study of 3,974 sampled roots they start 3% of the cascades that are not reply-led, 49% of those of 10,000 to 19,999 posts and 73% of those of 20,000+, and they hold 63% of the posts in cascades of 10,000+. Nearly all are run by companies (96% of those sampled). On the two full days their retweets are timed as chance predicts for their rate and fall off at night: the participants are people, drawn by the prize. The plan shown first keeps a random 20%. A prize draw is recognised from the root’s text, which search returns with the root, so one that is left out is not read or fetched. Which simulated cascades are prize draws is set by size alone, from the study’s shares: the plans show what leaving them out frees, not which cascades they are.
    • Estimating a size before the replies are read. By counts: a root read as reply-led is taken as its counts plus 4.25 times its replies; any other root, as its counts alone. By stream: 100 times the cascade's posts in the 1% sample, which needs each sampled post matched to its cascade and has not been tried.
    • Finding by the stream is not offered as a plan. The 1% stream samples at least one post of nearly every cascade of 300+ posts, so it could find the cascades the floors miss, and it is the natural check on them. Its cost cannot be worked out here: every cascade under 100 posts with a post or two in the stream would also have to be looked up, and those cascades are not in the files.
    • Costs are in units; one unit is the charge for one post returned. Each search pass is charged one unit for every root returned, taken as every root that passes a floor by the end of the window. Replies and other posts that pass the same floors, and lookups, are not costed. A shorter window scales a cascade's replies, quotes and root counts in step with its size. Buying the first N retweets, or a random sample of them, has not been tried; search returns the newest posts first, so either may cost more than shown.
    • How much the cost moves. Worked on the real cascades day by day, the opening plan costs 3.0M units for an average day's roots, from 2.4M to 3.6M, and 89M for the 30 days. The dearest seven days in a row (roots of 8 to 14 September) ran at a rate of 96M a month: more than the 90M the plan allows itself, and under the cap only because of the reserve. One month has been measured, so how much months differ is not known. A month with more volume than September, or 31 days, costs more: set the change in volume to see it. Other months scale September by their total.
    • Not tested. Nothing here has been tested on a paid account. Four things are assumed: that search filters on a post's counts at the time of the query, that a post's reply count is its direct replies, that the API's counts match the archive's, and what a repeated pass is charged.
    • The stream-only design, for comparison, predicts at day 1 which cascades will pass 10,000 posts in seven days. Depending on the rule it finds most, not all, of the 3,206 and downloads 69M to 99M posts a month, wrong guesses included. Every post of all 3,206 is 60M. These figures are for seven days and original-post roots.

    The model behind the conversations

    The planner generates every conversation at random from one model with three parts. The charts below change as you move the model controls on the left.

    An original post (root) draws its direct replies

    Most roots that get any reply get exactly one. A small popular group gets tens, hundreds or thousands. Roots in the popular group sets how many roots are in that group; Reach of the most popular posts sets how large they get.

    Each reply is answered, or not

    A reply that gets an answer usually gets exactly one, and chains of single answers make a back-and-forth exchange. The chance is lower under a popular root and varies between conversations. The two get an answer controls and Difference between conversations set it.

    Now and then an exchange splits

    When an exchange stops, its last reply sometimes gets several replies at once. Each starts a new exchange. Side threads sets how often.

    The equations

    1. Direct replies to the root, K. K = ⌊eZ⌋. With probability w the root is ordinary and Z is Normal with mean −a1/t12 and standard deviation 1/t1; otherwise it is popular and Z is Normal with mean −a2/t22 and standard deviation 1/t2. Only Z ≥ 0 is kept, so K ≥ 1.
    2. How persistent the conversation is, θ. θ is Normal(0, 1), drawn once per conversation. Write x = ln K.
    3. A reply to the root gets exactly one reply with probability q1 = logistic(c0 − c1x + s1θ).
    4. A later reply, in exchange j, gets exactly one reply with probability q2 = logistic(μ − γx + σθ + τεj), where εj is Normal(0, 1), drawn once per exchange.
    5. When an exchange ends, its last reply splits with probability φ = κ / (1 + ζ(α)) into D ≥ 2 replies, with P(D ≥ d) = (d − 1)−α. After a reply to the root, κ = logistic(g0 + f1x) and α = 1 + (α0 − 1)K−b1. After a later reply, κ = logistic(f0 + f1x) and α = 1 + (α0 − 1)K−b2.

    logistic(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z). ζ is the Riemann zeta function, so κ is the average number of new exchanges started when one ends. It is always below 1, which keeps every conversation finite.

    How to read this

    • Conversations are drawn from the reply-tree model described above. The page opens on a working guess for Japanese X today. It is not a measurement: no measurement of Japanese reply trees after 2011 was found.
    • Charges are in units. One unit is the charge for one post on X's public price list; counts and other charges are converted at the prices on the left. Whether they draw on your quota depends on the contract. Nothing here has been tested on a paid account.
    • Monthly volume and conversation shape rest on different counts. The default volume is an estimate of Japanese-written replies; the shapes describe conversations under Japanese roots.
    • The plan shown is the best of those tried: root search, a reply sample, or both; with or without a size check; keeping every flagged conversation or a random share. Every plan downloads what it keeps in full, or up to the read limit. Skipping the very largest conversations would collect more conversations and miss the largest ones; no plan here does that. Sizes and thresholds are tried on a grid, so “smallest size” means the smallest size tried.
    • The model is static. A conversation is judged on its final size; waiting times, deletions and replies not tagged as Japanese are not modelled.
    • The best guess and its variants come from two desk reviews (GPT Pro and Claude Opus). In the second round each was given the first round’s targets, so their agreement is partly built in. Scenarios are what-if cases, not probability bounds. Low end and High end change only the share of popular roots, which also changes how many conversations are quiet-root.
    • Moving a model control starts a fresh simulation, whose results carry a few percent of sampling noise. The scenario buttons load larger, steadier runs.
